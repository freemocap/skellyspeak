## Shared writing guide: Latin script

**Owner:** shared writing-system content. **Existing reference:** `latin`.
**Explanation language:** English. **Sources:** [@unicode17_europe].

### How the writing works

Latin-script languages use letters, often in uppercase and lowercase forms.
Additional letters and marks distinguish the spelling of particular languages.
A familiar letter shape does not guarantee a familiar sound.

### Reading units

Letters can work together as a unit. A language guide explains which combinations
matter and how to read them. A mark may be part of the spelling distinction even
when it is small or placed above or below another shape.

### Examples to inspect

| Display | What to notice |
| --- | --- |
| A · a | Uppercase and lowercase forms |
| e · é | A mark changes the written form; its function is language-dependent |
| n · ñ | Do not assume a marked letter can be read as its unmarked neighbor |

### Continue in your language

Use the selected language's guide for its alphabet, letter names, sound patterns
and spelling conventions. This shared page deliberately makes no claim that a
Latin letter has a single pronunciation across languages.

**Reader assistance projection:** Preserve written distinctions and source text.
Resolve language-specific readings from the selected language content. A simplified
matching view is not pronunciation or spelling evidence.

## Language reading guide: Spanish letters and combinations

**Owner:** Spanish teaching content, composed with the Latin guide.
**Scope:** an introductory shared Spanish spelling guide, not a regional accent
model. **Sources:** [@rae_spanish_alphabet2026], [@rae_question_marks2026].

### Alphabet and combinations

Spanish has 27 alphabet letters, including ñ. The combinations ch and ll remain
part of Spanish spelling but are not separate alphabet letters. Alphabet order
and the units you hear are therefore different things.

### Examples to inspect

| Example | Meaning | Reading observation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| año | year | Keep ñ distinct from n |
| chico | boy | Notice the two-letter combination ch |
| llave | key | Notice ll; its pronunciation needs the selected variety's guidance |
| ¿Vienes? | Are you coming? | Opening and closing marks identify the question |

### What this page does not settle

This introductory page is not a full pronunciation course. Letter names, sound
examples, stress and accent-mark rules need their own reviewed sections. Audio,
if added later, must have an explicit language/variety context and separate
provenance; it cannot be inferred from a shared Latin letter table.

**Reader assistance projection:** Recognize that alphabet entries and multi-letter
reading units differ. Preserve ñ and punctuation. Do not infer pronunciation
quality from the presence of a spelling pattern.

## Contrast writing guide: Devanagari

**Owner:** shared writing-system content. **Existing reference:** `devanagari`.
**Sources:** [@unicode17_indic].

### How the writing works

Devanagari organizes consonants and vowel signs into written combinations. A
consonant's inherent vowel can be modified by a vowel sign or suppressed with a
virama. Signs may be displayed in a different visual position from their logical
order in the text.

### Examples to inspect

| Display | What to notice |
| --- | --- |
| क | A consonant letter |
| कि | A dependent vowel sign displayed to the left of the consonant |
| क् | The consonant with a virama |

### Continue in your language

The writing pattern alone does not determine the pronunciation of every word.
Use the selected language's reading guide for spoken realization. This page must
not impose Hindi pronunciation on other languages using Devanagari.

**Reader assistance projection:** Preserve full source-bound units and shaping.
Do not treat visual left-to-right positions as source character offsets. This
guide illustrates why the content shape must allow combinations, not only a list
of isolated alphabet letters. It does not add a language-specific matching rule.

## Skill: courtesy — Thank, apologize or respond politely

**Target:** Spanish; introductory broadly usable examples, no variety override.
**Sources:** [@rae_gracias2026].

### How to use it

A short conventional expression can complete a social action. You do not need a
long sentence to thank someone. Choose wording for the actual exchange rather
than adding a polite-looking word to unrelated content.

### Patterns and examples

| Example | Meaning | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gracias. | Thank you. | Acknowledges help or a favor |
| Muchas gracias por tu ayuda. | Thank you very much for your help. | Names the reason for thanks |

### Boundaries

These examples illustrate thanking, not the entire range of apologies, welcomes
and polite responses covered by the shared skill. Additional sections can extend
this card without creating new scored skills. Merely discussing the word gracias
is different from using it to thank someone.

**Assessment guidance:** Count an appropriate conventional thanks as evidence even
when it is only a short utterance. Other valid polite responses may also satisfy
the shared criterion. Do not require these exact phrases or credit partner text.
Distinguish actual social use from quoted or metalinguistic mention using context.

## Skill: possession — Express possession

**Target:** Spanish. **Sources:** [@rae_possession2026].

### How to use it

Make the relationship between a person and a thing clear. Possessive wording can
also express relationships other than literal ownership, such as family ties.
The point is to identify the relevant relationship, not to use one required form.

### Patterns and examples

| Example | Meaning | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Es mi mochila. | It is my backpack. | mi identifies the speaker's relationship to the backpack |
| La mochila es de Ana. | The backpack belongs to Ana. | de Ana names its owner |
| Mi hermana vive aquí. | My sister lives here. | mi expresses a family relationship |

### Boundaries

A word related to owning something is not enough if the intended relationship is
unclear. Do not turn these examples into an exhaustive grammar checklist.

**Assessment guidance:** Accept possessive or other Spanish wording that makes
the relevant relation recoverable. Evaluate the current learner's meaning, with
context only to resolve references. Do not require both a possessive and a de
phrase, or treat literal ownership as the only possible relation.

## Skill: property — Attribute a property

**Target:** Spanish. **Sources:** [@umass_spanish_copulas2026].

### How to use it

Describe a quality or state of a person or thing. Spanish commonly uses ser or
estar with a description. The choice can change the interpretation; a simple
“permanent versus temporary” rule is not a complete account.

### Patterns and examples

| Example | Meaning | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| La mesa es grande. | The table is big. | Attributes size to the table |
| La puerta está abierta. | The door is open. | Describes the door's state |

### Boundaries

This skill asks whether a quality or state is conveyed. Choosing an imperfect form
does not automatically erase an understandable description. More precise form
feedback belongs alongside the assessment, not in a hidden replacement criterion.

**Assessment guidance:** Judge whether the intended referent and quality/state
are clear. The examples are common constructions, not required templates. Record
uncertainty or partial expression where meaning is unclear; distinguish that from
a recoverable property expressed with a local form error.

## Skill: future_reference — Refer to a future event

**Target:** Spanish. **Sources:** [@rae_future2026].

### How to use it

Locate an event after the relevant present. Spanish can use a future form or an
ir a + infinitive construction. The surrounding message helps establish the time
being discussed.

### Patterns and examples

| Example | Meaning | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mañana voy a cocinar. | Tomorrow I am going to cook. | Locates cooking tomorrow |
| Cocinaré mañana. | I will cook tomorrow. | Uses a future form with an explicit time |

### Boundaries

Not every future-form verb refers to future time: Spanish also uses future forms
for conjecture. Recognize the meaning in context rather than rewarding an ending.

**Assessment guidance:** Accept wording that actually places an event after the
reference time. Do not require a morphological future. A future-looking form or
isolated time word is not sufficient without recoverable temporal meaning.

## Skill: negation — Express negation

**Target:** Spanish. **Sources:** [@rae_negative_concord2026].

### How to use it

Say what is not the case. Spanish permits negative words to combine without
cancelling each other: a negative word after the verb can occur with no before it.

### Patterns and examples

| Example | Meaning | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No compro pan. | I do not buy bread. | Negates buying bread |
| No veo a nadie. | I do not see anyone. | Combines no and nadie |
| Nadie responde. | Nobody answers. | Places the negative subject before the verb |

### Boundaries

Do not apply an English-style prohibition on “double negatives.” Negating a whole
claim and precisely distinguishing several possible scopes are different demands;
the latter has its own shared skill.

**Assessment guidance:** Accept Spanish negative concord. Judge the intended
negative meaning rather than counting negative words. Mere absence of an action
in the learner's message is not a demonstrated negation.

## Skill: question — Ask a question

**Target:** Spanish. **Sources:** [@rae_question_marks2026].

### How to use it

Ask for confirmation or missing information. Standard Spanish writing surrounds
a direct question with opening and closing question marks. The requested
information must still be understandable.

### Patterns and examples

| Example | Meaning | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ¿Tienes agua? | Do you have water? | Requests confirmation |
| ¿Dónde está la estación? | Where is the station? | Requests a location |

### Boundaries

Question punctuation is useful writing guidance, but punctuation alone does not
establish that the learner has asked an intelligible question. Transcripts may
omit punctuation; that does not prove the speaker omitted the question.

**Assessment guidance:** Judge whether the requested confirmation or information
is recoverable. Do not require English question order or treat missing transcript
punctuation as failed communicative performance. Quoted partner questions are not
learner evidence. Do not infer listening or pronunciation ability from a transcript.
